# Mahler Symphony No. 8 — The Two Texts

Mahler's Symphony No. 8 in E-flat major ("Symphony of a Thousand") sets **two completely different texts** written roughly a thousand years apart. Mahler himself wrote to his wife Alma:

> *"The two parts are mutually explanatory… the spirit of creation and redemption are one and the same."*

The unifying theme is **redemption** through divine love (Agape):

| Part | Text | Era | Role |
|------|------|-----|------|
| I | *Veni, Creator Spiritus* (Latin hymn) | 9th c., Hrabanus Maurus | The soul **calls** for divine inspiration and light |
| II | Closing scenes of Goethe's *Faust* (German) | 19th c., Goethe | The soul **receives** that redemption through the Eternal Feminine |

> **Structural note:** the Part I / Part II split is also a **collective / individual** split. Part I has no solo roles at all — the Latin hymn is sung entirely by the two mixed choirs, the children's choir, and supported by the orchestra. Part II is where Mahler's 8 solo voices come in (Pater Ecstaticus, Pater Profundus, Doctor Marianus, the three penitent women, Gretchen, and the voiceless Mater Gloriosa). The deliberate structural and dramatic logic: Part I = divine / collective (Creator Spirit, no individual), Part II = human / individual (Faust, Gretchen, individual souls). See `mahler-8-part-2-roles.md` for the full Part II cast.

## Part I — *Veni, Creator Spiritus* (Mahler's Setting)

### What Mahler Changed

Mahler took considerable liberties with the standard 9th-century Latin hymn:

1. **Reordered the verses** — the standard liturgical sequence is broken up; for example, Verse 1's second couplet ("Imple superna gratia") is moved into the *secondary section* of the exposition rather than standing with Verse 1's first couplet.
2. **Exchanged couplets** in one verse.
3. **Replaced some words** with synonyms (e.g., Mahler's text reads "Vitemus omne noxium" where the standard reads "Vitemus omne pessimum").
4. **Used only the first half of one verse** ("Dissolve litis vincula / Adstringe pacis foedera"), placing it in the recapitulation for the thought of *peace*.

The text Mahler set contains **seven and a half verses** of the hymn, distributed non-contiguously across the movement's sonata-allegro sections. A page formerly owned by Bruno Walter (now in the New York Public Library) shows the Latin text Mahler wrote down before composing — Mahler based his setting on this list, with the modifications above.

### ⭐ Key Part — The Double Fugue Climax

Mahler's musical setting peaks in the development section where he launches a colossal **double fugue** at *"Accende lumen sensibus / Infunde amorem cordibus"*. The Accende breakthrough happens in the **third development section** (mm. 262–289), and the formal **double fugue** is in the **fifth development section** (mm. 312–365). Mahler weaves two ideas simultaneously:

> ### ✨ *Accende lumen sensibus,*
> ### ✨ *infunde amorem cordibus,*
> ### ✨ *infirma nostri corporis,*
> ### ✨ *virtute firmans perpeti.*

> **Kindle light in our senses,**
> **pour love into our hearts,**
> **and with enduring power**
> **strengthen the weakness of our flesh.**

**Why Mahler centers the movement here:**

- **Textual contrast** — two petitions are stacked at once: the prayer for *light* ("Accende") alongside the plea to *pour love* ("Infunde").
- **Musical energy** — the third development section (mm. 262–289) sets these two verses in a **radiant E major fortissimo**, with sudden upward impetus (*mit plötzlichem Aufschwung*); the fifth development section (mm. 312–365) then subjects both verses to a **grand double fugue** with marchlike character.
- **Symbolic anchor** — the falling-fourth **"Veni" motif** (E♭ → B♭) is inverted, stretched, and recombined here, making this stanza the generative core of the whole symphony.
- **Bridge function** — per Mahler's own statement (Munich 1910 dress rehearsal, transmitted by Webern): *"At the passage 'Accende lumen sensibus', the bridge extends over to the close of Faust. The passage is the pivotal point of the entire work."* The Accende theme becomes the **principal theme of Part II**.

## Part II — Goethe's *Faust*, Closing Scene (German)

Part II sets the final pages of Goethe's *Faust*: rocky mountain gorges (*Bergschluchten*), hermits, choirs of angels, redeemed penitents, and Faust's immortal soul ascending. The structure is dramatic / operatic rather than sonata-form.

Below are the excerpts Mahler actually sets, beginning with the principal solo voices and ending with the Chorus Mysticus.

### Pater Ecstaticus (High Tenor) — *"Ewiger Wonnebrand"*

```text
Ewiger Wonnebrand,
Glühendes Liebeband,
Siedender Schmerz der Brust,
Schäumende Gotteslust!

```

> Eternal brand of bliss,
> glowing bond of love,
> scalding pain of the breast,
> frothing rapture in God!

### Pater Profundus (Bass) — The Mountain Depths

```text
Wenn im Sturm der Felsenwand
Wasser sich zum Staub zerschlägt,
Wenn aus engen Felsenspalten
Quellen sich gewaltsam drängen,
Wenn mit ungeheurem Flüchten
Berg und Tal sich öffnen, schließen…

```

> When in the storm the rocky wall
> dashes the waters into spray,
> when from narrow rocky clefts
> springs force themselves forth,
> when with headlong, monstrous flight
> mountain and valley open and close…

### The Repentant Women (Trio of Sops & Altos)

Mary Magdalene, the Samaritan Woman, and Mary of Egypt plead together:

```text
Bei der Liebe, die den Füßen
Deines göttlichen Sohns
Ungeträufelt Salböl war,
Bei der Liebe, die zu dir
Aus der Wüste, staubgekleidet,
Pilgerin, den Weg sich fand…

Du, Maria Aegyptiaca,
Du erkanntest bald den Heiland
In dem Bruder, den du suchtest,
Tausend Schmerzen unterm Joche
Trübsal brachtest du der Tugend…

```

> By the love whose ointment flowed
> on the feet of your divine Son,
> by the love which, dust-clad,
> came pilgrim-wise to you from the desert…
>
> You, Mary of Egypt,
> you soon knew the Savior
> in the brother whom you sought,
> a thousand sorrows in the yoke…
> (Excerpt: Latin intercession "Ave, fons pietatis…")

### Gretchen / *Una Poenitentium* (Soprano)

```text
Neige, neige,
Du Ohnegleiche,
Du Urbild aller Gnaden,
Lass mein Flehen
Zu dir dringen,
Die du mich Tote wieder belebt hast…

```

> Bend, O bend,
> you who are without equal,
> you archetype of all grace,
> let my prayer
> reach up to you,
> you who have given me, the dead one, life again…

### Mater Gloriosa (Soprano) — a single line, floating high in strings and harp

```text

```

> Come! Come! rise up!
> Come! Come!

### Chorus Mysticus — the closing hymn

```text
Alles Vergängliche
Ist nur ein Gleichnis;
Das Unzulängliche,
Hier wird's Ereignis;
Das Unbeschreibliche,
Hier ist's getan;

```

> All that is transitory
> is merely a symbol;
> what is unattainable
> here becomes event;
> what is indescribable
> here is done;
> the Eternal Feminine
> draws us onward and upward.

### ⭐ Key Part — The Chorus Mysticus

The final hymn is **the climactic synthesis of the entire symphony**. Mahler sets it as the resolution to everything Part I called for:

> ### ✨ *Alles Vergängliche*
> ### ✨ *ist nur ein Gleichnis;*
> ### ✨ *das Unzulängliche,*
> ### ✨ *hier wird's Ereignis;*
> ### ✨ *das Unbeschreibliche,*
> ### ✨ *hier ist's getan;*
> ### ✨ *Das Ewig-Weibliche*
> ### ✨ *zieht uns hinan.*

> **All that is transitory is merely a symbol;**
> **what is unattainable becomes event here;**
> **what is indescribable is done here;**
> **the Eternal Feminine draws us upward.**

**Why Mahler centers the symphony here:**

- **Structural closure** — the choir enters *piano-pianissimo*, completely unaccompanied, then builds with harps, organ, and soaring strings into the loudest climax in classical music.
- **Leitmotif return** — at the peak, the offstage brass blasts the original **"Veni" motif** (the falling fourth) over the full double chorus, organ, and orchestra in blazing E-flat major. The plea for creative spirit in Part I is now fully answered.
- **Doctrinal core** — *"Das Ewig-Weibliche"* (the Eternal Feminine) is Goethe's shorthand for the redeeming power of divine, self-giving love (Agape). In one image, the symphony's two-thousand-year arc collapses into a single verb: *zieht uns hinan* — draws us upward.

## How the Two Verses Mirror Each Other

| Part I (Latin) | Part II (German) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *Veni, Creator Spiritus* — "Come, Creator Spirit" | Mater Gloriosa — *"Komm! steige herauf!"* — "Come! rise up!" | Call and answer |
| *Accende lumen sensibus* — "Kindle light in our senses" | *Blicket auf!* — "Look upward!" (Doctor Marianus) | Light revealed vs. light invoked |
| *Infunde amorem cordibus* — "Pour love into our hearts" | *Das Ewig-Weibliche zieht uns hinan* — "The Eternal Feminine draws us upward" | Love poured out vs. love completed |
| *Infirma nostri corporis / virtute firmans* — "Strengthen our weak flesh" | Faust's mortal body is shed; soul ascends | Weakness answered by redemption |

### Appendix Source Notes

- **German text** — Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, *Faust: Der Tragödie zweiter Teil* (1832). Source: Project Gutenberg eBook #2230, public domain. URL: `https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2230`.
- **English text** — Bayard Taylor's translation, *Goethe's Faust, Part II* (1870–71, public domain). Source: Internet Archive identifier `goethetaylorfaust02`, full-text search export. URL: `https://archive.org/details/goethetaylorfaust02`.
