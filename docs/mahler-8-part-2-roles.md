# Mahler 8, Part II — The Eight Solo Roles

The vocal soloists of Part II of Mahler's Symphony No. 8, the story of *Faust* they come from, and how they relate to one another.

---

## 1. The Story of Faust (what you need to know)

Mahler's Part II sets the **closing scene** of Goethe's *Faust, Part Two* — called *Bergschluchten* ("Rocky Gorges" or "Mountain Clefts"). To understand why these eight characters are present and what they are doing, you need the broader story.

### 1.1 *Faust, Part One* (1808 / 1829)

**Dr. Heinrich Faust**, an aging scholar in medieval Germany, has mastered everything academic knowledge can offer and is nevertheless in despair. He attempts suicide, hears church bells at Easter, and is instead visited by **Mephistopheles** (the Devil in disguise). Mephistopheles proposes a wager: he will give Faust any experience he desires; in return, if Faust ever experiences a moment so perfect that he wishes it to last forever, he belongs to Mephistopheles.

Faust accepts. His first great experience is the seduction of **Margarete (Gretchen)** — a young, innocent, deeply religious woman. The seduction and abandonment are catastrophic:

- Faust and Mephistopheles leave Gretchen pregnant
- Faust gives Gretchen a sleeping potion to give her mother; the mother dies from an overdose
- Gretchen's brother **Valentine** confronts Faust; Faust (with Mephistopheles' help) kills Valentine in a duel
- Gretchen, in madness, drowns her newborn child and is condemned to death for infanticide
- Faust does not accompany her to prison; he is dragged away by Mephistopheles to other adventures
- From her prison cell, Gretchen voices forgiveness and appeal to heaven: *"Heinrich! Mir graut's vor dir"* — but with a glimmer of hope in God's mercy

This is the human tragedy that haunts the whole of *Faust Part Two*.

### 1.2 *Faust, Part Two* (1832, published posthumously)

Years pass. Faust returns from classical antiquity (where he has wooed **Helen of Troy** as an embodiment of pure beauty) and devotes himself to a political project: draining a swamp on the emperor's behalf to create land for a free people. He is now over 100 years old and blind, but still striving.

**The death of Faust**:

Faust is standing on the edge of the drained land, imagining his people in their new home, when he feels a moment of profound satisfaction. Care (a spirit of worry) whispers to him, and Faust utters the fateful word: *"Verweile doch, du bist so schön"* — "Stay, you are so fair." Mephistopheles, hearing this, claims Faust's soul per the pact.

But the angels intervene. They scatter Mephistopheles' crew and carry Faust's soul heavenward. The angelic choir sings the famous verse:

> *"Gerettet ist das edle Glied*
> *Der Geisterwelt vom Bösen:*
> *Wer immer strebend sich bemüht,*
> *Den können wir erlösen,*
> *Und hat an ihm die Liebe gar*
> *Von oben teilgenommen,*
> *Begegnet ihm die selige Schar*
> *Mit herzlichem Willkommen."*
>
> "Rescued is the noble member
> of the spirit-world from evil.
> Whoever strives with constant effort,
> Him we can redeem.
> And if love has touched him even
> from above,
> The blessed host meets him
> with a hearty welcome."

The reasoning: Faust never *did* achieve satisfaction in a way that would damn him — he was imagining the *future* of his people, not resting in the present. The angels carry his immortal essence upward.

### 1.3 The closing scene — *Bergschluchten* (Act V)

The remainder of *Faust, Part Two* is this mountain-gorge scene. It is **not** narrative drama but a **liturgical ascent**. Faust's soul rises through layers of spiritual beings — anchorites (hermits), angels, redeemed women — until it reaches the Mater Gloriosa and the highest heaven.

The scene is constructed as a **vertical hierarchy** of spiritual beings, ascending from the rocky depths to the divine heights. Each character has a specific role in welcoming, interceding for, or transmitting Faust's soul.

This is the scene Mahler set to music in the second part of his symphony.

## 2. The Eight Solo Roles

> **Part I has no solo roles.** The Latin *Veni, Creator Spiritus* hymn of Part I is sung entirely by the two mixed choirs, the children's choir, and supported by the orchestra — there is no solo vocal writing in Part I at all. All eight solo roles belong to Part II alone. The file `mahler-8-analysis.md` §7 covers Part I's structure (sonata-allegro with coda on the *Veni* text); this file covers only the Part II soloists.

### 2.1 Pater Ecstaticus — "the Ecstatic Father" (High Tenor)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **12** *Ewiger Wonnebrand* (Part II 0:00 → 15:05). Also referenced instrumentally in Track **11** (the orchestral transition mm. ~280–384, where horn + trumpet foreshadow the *Accende* theme that he later sings against).

**Who he is**: One of the **holy anchorites** — Christian hermits living in caves and wilderness in the mountains above the rocky gorges. The Pater Ecstaticus lives at a middle elevation, hovering in spiritual ecstasy.

**What he sings** (the famous *Ewiger Wonnebrand* aria):

> *"Ewiger Wonnebrand,*
> *Glühendes Liebeband,*
> *Siedender Schmerz der Brust,*
> *Schäumende Gotteslust!"*
>
> "Eternal brand of bliss,
> Glowing bond of love,
> Seething pain of the breast,
> Frothing rapture in God!"

He then prays to be struck through — pierced by arrows, lances, clubs, lightning — *"dass ja das Nichtige / alles verfüchte"* — "so that what is empty may vanish entirely" — and that the *Dauerstern, ewiger Liebe Kern* (the lasting star, the core of eternal love) may shine forth.

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: A soaring tenor aria marked *sehr feurig, leidenschaftlich* (very fiery, passionate). Mahler divides it into five musical periods of eight measures each, all beginning with the same motif, giving it a da-capo character. The music is "quite songlike" and anticipates the late-Romantic *Lieder* style. It uses the **Accende theme** (the Part-I double-fugue subject) instrumentally, rounding off the Pater's song.

**Relationship to the rest**: He is the first solo voice to break the orchestral silence of Part II's opening (the *Poco Adagio / instrumental Arioso*). His ecstatic song establishes the spiritual register of Part II. The Pater Profundus enters after him as the contrasting depth-register.

---

### 2.2 Pater Profundus — "the Profound Father" (Bass)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **13** *Wie Felsenabgrund mir zu Füßen* (Part II 15:05 → 19:04). The *Accende* theme is used instrumentally in three marked passages within this track.

**Who he is**: Another of the holy anchorites, but dwelling in the **lowest** elevation — in caves deep within the mountain. He represents the lower, earthbound stratum of male spiritual life.

**What he sings** (his aria, set as an *Allegro appassionato*):

> *"Wenn im Sturm der Felsenwand*
> *Wasser sich zum Staub zerschlägt,*
> *Wenn aus engen Felsenspalten*
> *Quellen sich gewaltsam drängen…"*
>
> "When in the storm the rocky wall
> dashes the waters into spray,
> when from narrow rocky clefts
> springs force themselves forth…"

He sings of the most violent natural forces — storms, torrents, earthquakes, the eternal war of elements — and concludes that all of nature, even in its violence, follows **divine love**:

> *"So ist es die allmächtige Liebe,*
> *die alles durchdringt,*
> *die alles bildet,*
> *die alles belebt…"*
>
> "Thus it is love almighty
> that penetrates all things,
> forms all things,
> quickens all things…"

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: An *Allegro appassionato* Arioso in **E-flat minor** — the deep-earth register. The orchestration features heavy brass, stormy low strings, and brooding rhythms, in deliberate contrast to the Pater Ecstaticus's lighter, brighter music. Mahler uses the Accende theme for **three** passages of the Pater Profundus's music — three passages that speak emphatically of love and enlightenment. He marks them with special performance instructions: *gehalten und leidenschaftlich* (sustained and passionate), *maestoso*, and *mit Inbrunst* (with fervor).

**Relationship to the rest**: He is the bass-register counterpart to Pater Ecstaticus. Together the two patres establish the full male spiritual range — ecstasy above, depth below. Their contrast prepares the way for Doctor Marianus, who speaks from the highest spiritual register.

---

### 2.3 Doctor Marianus (Tenor)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Tracks **17** *Ich spür…* (23:47 → 24:20), **18** *Hier ist die Aussicht frei — Freudig empfangen wir* (24:20 → 24:56, his first address to the Mater Gloriosa + immediate response by the blessed boys), **19** *Höchste Herrscherin der Welt* (24:56 → 27:10, his praise of the Mater Gloriosa), **20** *Jungfrau, rein im schönsten Sinne* (27:10 → 28:38, Mater Gloriosa invocation continued), and **31** *Blicket auf zum Retterblick* (42:16 → 47:45, his hymn + the closing *diminuendo* postlude).

**Who he is**: The **highest-ranked** of the holy anchorites — a religious scholar and contemplative. He is positioned at the top of the mountain hierarchy among the hermits, looking up toward heaven.

**What he sings**:

> *"Du, dem es wohl zu allen Zeiten*
> *Gott anvertraut, des neuen Bundes Schlüssel*
> *Und als Stellvertreter nebenbei*
> *Der Gottheit höchste Lieb' und Lust zu künden…"*
>
> "You who at all times
> God has trusted with the keys of the new covenant
> And as representative to proclaim
> The highest love and delight of the Godhead…"

He then addresses the Virgin Mary directly:

> *"Höchste Herrscherin der Welt,*
> *Lasse mich im blauen*
> *Ausgespannten Himmelszelt*
> *Dein Geheimnis schauen."*
>
> "Highest ruler of the world,
> Let me in the blue
> Outstretched tent of heaven
> Gaze upon your mystery."

And later, addressing all mortals:

> *"Blicket auf! Die Wunde klafft*
> *Mitten eures Herzens…"*
>
> "Look up! The wound gapes
> In the midst of your hearts…"

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: A tenor part in **E major** (the breakthrough key) at his address to the Mater Gloriosa — marked *"Höchste Herrscherin der Welt!"*. Mahler derives the melodic figure from the **Accende theme**. The praise modulates from E major to E-flat major at the words *"wenn du uns befriedest"* ("when you pacify us") — the moment of alleviation. The postlude on the *Blicket auf* motif uses glockenspiel and tam-tam and ends with *diminuendo bis zum Verlöschen* (diminuendo to the point of dying away).

**Relationship to the rest**: Doctor Marianus is the **highest male spiritual figure** in the symphony. He directs the action: addressing the Mater Gloriosa, pointing heavenward with *"Blicket auf!"*, and his hymn introduces and supports the Mater Gloriosa's appearance. His music is the bridge between the masculine contemplative realm (the patres) and the feminine divine realm (Mater Gloriosa).

---

### 2.4 Magna Peccatrix — "the Great Sinner" (Mary Magdalene)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **23** *Bei der Liebe, die den Füßen* (32:12 → 33:07). Opens the trio of penitent women bracketed by the Mater Gloriosa theme.

**Who she is**: The first of the **three holy penitent women** — *Die drei heiligen Büßerinnen*. In Christian tradition, Mary Magdalene was a sinner who repented and became one of Christ's most devoted followers. She is traditionally identified (in Western Christianity) with the woman who anointed Christ's feet, with Mary of Bethany (Lazarus's sister), and with the woman caught in adultery whom Christ forgave.

**What she sings**:

> *"Bei der Liebe, die den Füßen*
> *Deines göttlichen Sohns*
> *Ungeträufelt Salböl war…"*
>
> "By the love whose ointment flowed
> On the feet of your divine Son…"

She appeals to the Virgin Mary by invoking the love that anointed Christ's feet — her own act of devotion.

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: *Fließend* ("flowing"). Song style (not hymn or chorale), in B major opening to E-flat major closing. Her music is **bracketed by the Mater Gloriosa theme** — it begins with her theme and closes with her theme. This is the first appearance of the Mater Gloriosa theme in Part II.

**Relationship to the rest**: She is the **first of the three penitent women** and the one with the most direct personal connection to Christ (anointing his feet). She is grouped with Mulier Samaritana and Maria Aegyptiaca as a trio of biblical women who represent **mercy that overcomes sin**. Her song leads directly into the Samaritan Woman's song.

---

### 2.5 Mulier Samaritana — "the Samaritan Woman" (Soprano or Alto)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **24** *Bei dem Bronn, zu dem schon weiland* (33:07 → 34:31). Second of the three penitent women.

**Who she is**: The second penitent woman. In the Gospel of John (chapter 4), she is the woman at Jacob's well whom Christ asked for water; she had had five husbands and was living with a man not her husband. Christ revealed her past to her, she confessed, and she became a missionary in her city (Sychar).

**What she sings**:

> *"Du, überreicher Gott,*
> *Wie soll ich dich preisen…"*
>
> "You, God over-rich,
> How shall I praise you…"

She invokes Christ's gift of the living water — the well that becomes a spring of eternal life.

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: *Immer dasselbe Tempo* ("always the same tempo") — a continuation of the song-style. Opening in E-flat minor, closing in E-flat major. The dark-to-light modulation in her own aria mirrors the larger dark-to-light arc of Part II.

**Relationship to the rest**: She is the **middle voice of the trio** — chronologically between Mary Magdalene (the anointing) and Mary of Egypt (the desert withdrawal). Her confession at the well is the moment of personal acknowledgment that precedes true repentance.

---

### 2.6 Maria Aegyptiaca — "Mary of Egypt" (Soprano or Alto)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **25** *Bei dem hochgeweihten Orte* (34:31 → 35:33). Third and final penitent woman.

**Who she is**: The third penitent woman. According to medieval legend (recorded by Sophronius of Jerusalem in the 7th century and popular in the medieval West), Mary of Egypt was a prostitute who followed pilgrims to Jerusalem intending to seduce them at the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, was miraculously turned away by an invisible force, repented, lived as a desert hermit for 47 years, and was found at the moment of her death by the monk Zosimas.

**What she sings**:

> *"Lasse mich, du geistlich' Gewand,
> *In der Wüste knien…"*
>
> "Let me, you spiritual garment,
> Kneel in the desert…"

She prays to be allowed to kneel in the desert, mourning her sins.

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: *Immer fließend* ("always flowing"). Song style in G minor opening to E-flat major closing. The G-minor opening is the darkest of the three women — her aria opens in the lowest, most penitent key, then ascends.

**Relationship to the rest**: She is the **third and last** of the trio. Her story is the most complete withdrawal from the world — from prostitute to desert hermit — and her song represents the most extreme form of repentance. After her song, the **three women sing together** (the *Trio*).

---

### 2.7 Gretchen / Una Poenitentium — "Gretchen, the Penitent One" (Soprano)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** Track **27** *Neige, neige, du Ohnegleiche* (37:10 → 37:50, her solo prayer to the Mater Gloriosa), Track **29** *Vom edlen Geisterchor umgeben* (39:05 → 41:08, leads the choir of penitent women; "Er ahnet kaum das frische Leben"), Track **30** *Komm! hebe dich zu höhern Sphären* (41:08 → 42:16, asks the Mater Gloriosa to teach Faust, after which the Mater's "Komm!" reply follows).

**Who she is**: **Margarete (Gretchen)** from *Faust, Part One* — Faust's former lover, the innocent girl whose life he destroyed. By the time of the *Bergschluchten*, she has been **redeemed**: she has repented, she has died (or ascended) and joined the realm of the blessed. She is called *Una Poenitentium* — "one of the repentant" — placing her within the category of the penitent women, but as a singular figure she has her own distinct identity and role.

**What she sings**:

> *"Neige, neige,*
> *Du Ohnegleiche,*
> *Du Urbild aller Gnaden,*
> *Lass mein Flehen*
> *Zu dir dringen,*
> *Die du mich Tote wieder belebt hast…"*
>
> "Bend, O bend,
> You who are without equal,
> You archetype of all grace,
> Let my prayer
> Reach up to you,
> You who have given me, the dead one, life again…"

She then turns to Faust (now a soul being elevated) and describes his transformation:

> *"Er ahnet kaum das frische Leben,*
> *So gleicht er schon der heiligen Schar.*
> *Sieh, wie er jedem Erdenbande*
> *Der alten Hülle sich entrafft,
> *Und aus ätherischem Gewande*
> *Hervortritt erste Jugendkraft!"*
>
> "He hardly senses the fresh, new life,
> So he is already resembling the sacred host.
> See how he departs from every earthly bond,
> From the old husk,
> And out of an ethereal garment
> Steps forth fresh youthful strength!"

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: Her key aria is built on music **borrowed from Part I** (mm. 46–61, 108–122) — the *"Imple superna gratia"* music — because the texts of both passages express the thought of **grace**. She is accompanied by the **mandolin** (only used during her aria) — Mahler attached symbolic meaning to this instrument (it appears only during the Una Poenitentium's music, fitting her reference to the *"früh Geliebten, nicht mehr Getrübten"* — the early beloved, no longer saddened one). Her aria opens in D major (a brighter, more pastoral mode); her closing prayer in the *dolcissimo* Mater Gloriosa section returns to E-flat major.

**Relationship to the rest**: She is the **most personal** of the eight soloists — Faust's individual connection to the realm of grace. Where the three penitent women plead from their own historical/legendary sins, Gretchen pleads specifically for Faust. She has **direct biographical connection to the protagonist** (whom the others do not know personally) and her intercession is the **most immediate and specific**.

---

### 2.8 Mater Gloriosa — "the Glorious Mother" (Soprano)

> **🎧 Dudamel (DG 2021):** She is **voiceless** throughout the recording — represented only by orchestral sound. Establishes as a presence in Track **21** *Äußerst langsam. Adagissimo* (28:38 → 30:09, the solo violin + harp + harmonium *Religioso* texture); the theme brackets the penitent-women trio in Tracks **22** (30:09 → 32:12), **26** (35:33 → 37:10), and **27** (37:10 → 37:50); she is invoked by Doctor Marianus in Track **20** (27:10 → 28:38) and by Gretchen in Track **30** (41:08 → 42:16). Her single spoken line — "Komm! Komm! steige herauf!" — is heard during the bridge from Track **30** into Track **31**.

**Who she is**: The **Virgin Mary** — the Mother of God in Catholic devotion. She is the highest feminine figure in the entire symphony, the divine feminine par excellence. In Catholic theology, she is the *Mediatrix* — the one who mediates between the penitent soul and her Son.

**What she sings**: A single line. Just two words, repeated:

> *"Komm! Komm! steige herauf!*
> *Komm! Komm!"*
>
> "Come! Come! Rise upward!
> Come! Come!"

This is her **only** utterance in the symphony. She does not sing an aria; she does not address anyone specifically. She **summons**.

**Musical treatment in Mahler**: She enters in the **Äußerst langsam / Adagissimo** section (mm. 780ff.), *schwebend* ("floating"). Her music is in **E major** (the breakthrough key), modulating to **B major** (the dominant of E major). The orchestration features **harmonium** (entering at m. 780) and harps (first harp chord at m. 639 when Doctor Marianus addresses her) — Mahler reserved these instruments for her appearance. Her theme is derived from the **Accende theme** by "minor changes" — the same theme that has structured Part I and the Mater Gloriosa's address.

**Relationship to the rest**: She is the **summit** — above all the other feminine figures. Where the three penitent women *plead* and Gretchen *intercedes*, the Mater Gloriosa **summons and grants**. Her single line is the **act of redemption**: Faust's soul is permitted to ascend. She is the **last** solo voice before the chorus mysticus begins; after her floating, the chorus of the blessed young boys welcomes Faust's now-redeemed soul.

## 3. The Relationships — A Hierarchy

### 3.1 The Vertical Hierarchy

The eight soloists (plus the choral groups) form a **strictly vertical hierarchy** that mirrors the **upward movement** of Faust's soul through the spiritual realms.

```
                                                          Mater Gloriosa
                                                          (Virgin Mary)
                                                          — SUMMIT —
                                                          ↑
                                                  Gretchen / Una Poenitentium
                                                  — PERSONAL INTERCESSOR —
                                                  ↑
                                       Three Penitent Women (trio)
                                       Mary Magdalene + Samaritan + Mary of Egypt
                                       — HISTORICAL / LEGENDARY INTERCESSION —
                                       ↑
                            Doctor Marianus
                            — HIGHEST MALE SPIRITUALITY —
                            ↑
              Pater Ecstaticus            Pater Profundus
              (high tenor)                (bass)
              — ECSTASY ↑ —               — DEPTH ↓ —
              
              [boys' choir, angel choirs, chorus mysticus below and around]
```

### 3.2 The Three Male / Three Female / Two Bridge Structure

The eight soloists can be grouped in **three pairs and two bridges**:

| Group | Roles | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **The three Patres** | Pater Ecstaticus + Pater Profundus + Doctor Marianus | The **male spiritual hierarchy** — ecstasy / depth / highest contemplation |
| **The three Penitent Women** | Magna Peccatrix + Mulier Samaritana + Maria Aegyptiaca | The **female historical redemption** — biblical women whose sins were forgiven |
| **The personal intercessor** | Gretchen / Una Poenitentium | The **specific, personal** intercession for Faust (one of the penitent women, but unique) |
| **The summit** | Mater Gloriosa | The **divine feminine** — the one who grants redemption |

The three patres are also internally organized:

- Pater Ecstaticus = the **high / upward** spiritual life
- Pater Profundus = the **low / downward** spiritual life (but spiritually profound — note the paradoxical "depth" of the "Profundus")
- Doctor Marianus = the **highest** spiritual life (looking upward toward Mary)

The three penitent women are chronologically and personally:

- Mary Magdalene = the **early follower** of Christ (anointed his feet)
- The Samaritan Woman = the **living water** recipient (John 4)
- Mary of Egypt = the **desert penitent** (legendary, much later)

### 3.3 The Two "Ascending Lines"

There are **two ascending lines** that meet at the Mater Gloriosa:

**The male line**: Pater Ecstaticus → Pater Profundus → Doctor Marianus → Mater Gloriosa

This is the line of male contemplation that ascends from the heights (ecstasy) through the depths (nature) to the summit (the Virgin).

**The female line**: Mary Magdalene → Samaritan Woman → Mary of Egypt → (trio) → Gretchen/Una Poenitentium → Mater Gloriosa

This is the line of female intercession that ascends from biblical history through personal intercession (Gretchen) to the divine feminine.

Both lines **converge at the Mater Gloriosa**, who is the summit of both.

### 3.4 The Line of Ascent in the Source Chapter

The OCR'd source chapter on the Eighth (book page 237) summarizes the female line:

> *"From Gretchen → the Una Poenitentium → the three holy repenting women → the Mater Gloriosa → into the primeval light of divine [women], strength of love."*

> *"God, however, the highest light and will again be [seen as] godly, reveals itself as loving and feminine to human eyes. Therefore a line leads from the holy Gretchen to the Una Poenitentium, to the three holy repenting women, to the Mater Gloriosa, and into the primeval light of divine [women], strength of love."*

The line is **strictly vertical** — each level is higher than the previous, and the Mater Gloriosa is the threshold between the historical/specific and the eternal/universal.

## 4. How Mahler Uses Each Role Musically

### 4.1 Voice-type distribution

| Voice | Roles | Musical character |
|---|---|---|
| **Soprano** | Magna Peccatrix (sometimes alto), Mulier Samaritana (sometimes alto), Maria Aegyptiaca (sometimes alto), Gretchen, Mater Gloriosa | The five highest female parts — light, high, often a cappella or with light accompaniment |
| **Alto** | Some of the penitent women (depending on cast) | Blends with the sopranos in the trio |
| **Tenor** | Pater Ecstaticus, Doctor Marianus | The two higher male parts — Pater Ecstaticus hovering, Doctor Marianus assertive |
| **Bass** | Pater Profundus | The lowest solo part — deep, contemplative, nature-bound |

Note: the standard Mahler 8 cast uses **three sopranos, no alto, two tenors, one bass**. Some editions / productions use an alto for one of the penitent women to deepen the trio; this is a casting choice.

### 4.2 First appearances in musical time

| Order | Character | Musical entry | Key |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pater Ecstaticus | After the orchestral Arioso, soaring above | E-flat major |
| 2 | Pater Profundus | After Ecstaticus's song fades | E-flat minor |
| 3 | Doctor Marianus | After the angel choir, with glockenspiel | E major → E-flat major |
| 4 | Magna Peccatrix | Trio begins (after Mater Gloriosa has been invoked) | B major → E-flat major |
| 5 | Mulier Samaritana | Continuation of trio | E-flat minor → E-flat major |
| 6 | Maria Aegyptiaca | Final solo of trio | G minor → E-flat major |
| 7 | Gretchen | After the trio, with mandolin | D major → E-flat major |
| 8 | Mater Gloriosa | After Gretchen's prayer, floating | E major → B major |

The Mater Gloriosa's entry is therefore **last** among the soloists — she arrives when the intercession has reached its summit. Her single line is the **granting** of all that has been requested.

### 4.3 The defining accompaniment for each role

| Role | Defining accompaniment | Symbolic meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Pater Ecstaticus | Light woodwinds, harp | Spiritual elevation |
| Pater Profundus | Heavy brass, low strings, storm | Earthly depth, nature's power |
| Doctor Marianus | Glockenspiel, tam-tam (in his postlude) | The holy / contemplative; "Blicket auf!" |
| Magna Peccatrix | Harp + Mater Gloriosa theme bracket | Connection to the divine feminine |
| Mulier Samaritana | Continuation of trio texture | Personal confession |
| Maria Aegyptiaca | Continuation, with darker opening | The deepest penitent |
| Gretchen | **Mandolin** (only used here) | The "early beloved, no longer saddened" |
| Mater Gloriosa | **Harmonium, harps** (reserved for her) | The *Religioso*, the floating divine |

## 5. What Each Role Adds to the Drama

A concise summary of what each role **does** in the scene:

- **Pater Ecstaticus**: opens the spiritual register of Part II; establishes the ecstatic mode.
- **Pater Profundus**: opens the earthbound / nature register; shows that even the violent depths are permeated by divine love.
- **Doctor Marianus**: bridges the male and female realms; directs the action upward; introduces the Mater Gloriosa.
- **Magna Peccatrix**: opens the biblical intercession; invokes Christ through personal devotion (the anointing).
- **Mulier Samaritana**: continues the biblical intercession; invokes Christ through confession.
- **Maria Aegyptiaca**: closes the biblical intercession; invokes Christ through withdrawal into the desert.
- **Gretchen**: provides the **personal** intercession for Faust; her song explains what is happening to his soul.
- **Mater Gloriosa**: **grants redemption** with a single line; her appearance is the climax of the ascent.

## 6. Notes on What Is Not Sung

A few points worth knowing:

### 6.1 Faust himself does not sing

Faust is already dead by the *Bergschluchten* scene. His **immortal soul** is the subject of the entire scene, but he does not have a vocal line. His presence is mediated by:

- The **boys' choir** ("selige Knaben" / blessed young boys), who sing his spiritual reincarnation: *"Freudig empfangen wir / Diesen im Puppenstand"* ("Joyfully we receive him in a pupal state")
- The trumpet-and-horn entry at mm. 422–425 (before any text) which foreshadows the boys' choir theme
- The Mater Gloriosa's summons, which is addressed to him: "Komm!" — "Come!"

### 6.2 The Mater Gloriosa has only one line

She is the **highest** figure and the **least verbose**. By speaking only *"Komm!"*, she makes the single word the supreme act of redemption. In opera and oratorio, the most powerful divine figures tend to be the most concise (compare Christ in Bach's *St. Matthew Passion*, who speaks little).

### 6.3 The three penitent women are a trio

They sing sequentially as individual solos, then **together** as a trio (mm. 993–1141). The trio is unified by the **Mater Gloriosa theme** (which brackets the trio) and the **Rose chorus theme** (which returns within the trio — mm. 970–978 ↔ 474–482; mm. 1058–1079 ↔ 443–461).

### 6.4 The angelic and boys' choirs are *not* solo roles

For completeness:

- **Younger angels** (*Jüngere Engel*) — chorus, sings the *Jene Rosen* (Rose chorus / Scherzo)
- **More perfected angels** (*Vollendete Engel*) — chorus, sings *"Uns bleibt ein Erdenrest…"*
- **Blessed young boys** (*Selige Knaben*) — boys' choir (often a separate ensemble), sings *"Freudig empfangen wir"* and the later return of the same music (mm. 1187–1212)
- **Chorus Mysticus** — full chorus, sings the closing hymn

These choral roles are distinct from the eight soloists but interact with them musically and dramatically (e.g., the boys' choir's theme is foreshadowed by the trumpet/horn in the orchestral prelude).

              Mater Gloriosa
              (soprano, E major)         — one floating line: "Komm!"
                     ↑

                     ↑
              The Trio of Penitent Women

                     ↑

## 8. Sources

- **Goethe, Johann Wolfgang von**. *Faust. Zweiter Teil*. 1832 (posthumous). Modern critical edition: *Goethes Faust*, ed. Erich Trunz and others (Hamburger Ausgabe).
- **de la Grange, Henry-Louis**. *Gustav Mahler*, Vol. 3 — the source chapter on the Eighth (book pp. 213–237), used for the structural mapping of the soloists' roles and musical treatment.
- **Mitchell, Donald**. *Gustav Mahler: The Wunderhorn Years* — useful for Goethe context.
- The chapter references **AME** (Alma's memoirs) for Mahler's own gloss on the *Ewig-Weibliche* as the structuring principle of Part II.

For the biblical figures (Mary Magdalene, the Samaritan Woman, Mary of Egypt), the principal sources are:

- **The New Testament** (Gospels of John and Luke; the apocryphal *Acts of Pilate* / *Gospel of Nicodemus* for Mary Magdalene's later traditions)

- The medieval and Catholic traditions summarized in the *Butler's Lives of the Saints* and similar reference works.
